Why a household-controlled PHR, now

  • 63 million US adults are family caregivers (≈1 in 4), up 45–50% since 2015[1] — exactly the workload a record-keeping product must support.
  • 131 million US adults (51.4%) live with multiple chronic conditions; 78.8% of seniors do[2] — the highest-prevalence life stage and the group most exposed to care-coordination failures between PCP and specialists.
  • 13.6 million US children (18.5%) are Children With Special Health Care Needs, touching ≈1 in 4 households; only 14.6% receive care in a well-functioning system[3].
  • Prior consumer PHRs (Google Health, Microsoft HealthVault) failed not on demand but on manual-entry friction[4] — users routinely omit source, date, units[5]. Unified text/file/voice intake with AI classification removes that exact friction.
  • RAG-grounded medical Q&A is efficient and reduces hallucination, but current systems accept misinformation from open-web retrieval[6] — which validates grounding answers in a household-vetted wiki + the patient's own records, not the open internet.
  • Trust hinges on citations: physician and patient acceptance of LLM-generated medical summaries depends on verifiable references back to source records[7].
  • The regulatory wind is at our back: the 21st Century Cures Act + ONC 2015 Edition rule mandated patient API access[9], Mandl & Kohane framed patient-controlled repositories as a "tectonic shift"[10], and consumer PHRs from non-covered entities fall outside HIPAA[11] — a federal regulatory gap a self-hosted PHR uniquely closes.

The opportunity

Roughly 243 million Americans fall into at least one of these PHR-relevant cohorts. Care-coordination failures between PCP and specialists delay diagnosis for the comorbid[2]; only 14.6% of CSHCN families have a well-functioning system[3]. The 55% of family caregivers who already perform medical or nursing tasks at home[1] are an active, instrumented user base — they aren't waiting for a record system to find a problem to solve.

Why prior PHRs failed

Google Health was discontinued in 2011; Microsoft HealthVault wound down in 2019. Spil & Klein's grounded-theory analysis (51 interviews specifically on Google Health) identified two root causes: vendor neglect of perceived usefulness, and low user trust / high perceived risk[4]. Kim & Johnson directly observed that manual-entry PHR users "failed to account for the source, date, units, and limits of reference ranges"[5] — turning the records into noise instead of signal. AI voice transcription has documented errors on medication names specifically[8], which justifies a classification+validation layer rather than raw ASR text.

What's different about this product

Three differentiated capabilities, each tied to a documented failure mode:

  1. Unified Intake — one input box, any format. An AI agent classifies and normalizes free-form text / files / voice into 15 structured record types, restoring the metadata Kim & Johnson found patients omitted[5].
  2. Curated Knowledge Base + Cross-Record Q&A — a household-vetted wiki plus a read-only chat agent that grounds answers in actual records with citations, addressing both the open-web misinformation risk[6] and the citation-trust requirement[7].
  3. Multi-Channel Capture — Telegram + WhatsApp pairing meets caregivers at the point of friction (a parent's phone in a clinic waiting room), not at a desktop browser.

✓ pass Unified intake — paste text, records appear

The killer constraint for prior PHRs: users were expected to fill structured forms with the source, date, units, and reference ranges of every value[5]. Most didn't. Here, you paste plain English — "BP 138/88, HR 76, weight 82.5 kg this morning before breakfast" — and the intake agent classifies it, infers the measurement timestamp, and writes a structured vital_signs row. The metadata gets reconstructed, not transcribed.
What this proves: Removing the data-entry friction is the historical pivot. AI classification into 15 structured types reverses that.

✓ pass Unified intake — upload a lab PDF, structured test results appear

A lipid panel arrives as a PDF from LabCorp or Quest. The 21st Century Cures Act gives Alice the right to that data[9], but the document is still a printable artifact — total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, triglycerides, each with their own reference range. Upload it. The OCR layer (openai-codex/gpt-5.5) extracts the text, the intake agent parses every numbered row, the values land as structured test_results with reference ranges + flags preserved.
What this proves: Patient owns the data (per ONC's 2015 Edition + Cures Act API mandate[9]); AI does the parsing. The reference-range and units metadata that Kim & Johnson found patients omit[5] is reconstructed from the PDF itself.

✓ pass Unified intake — speak a memo, records appear

A caregiver in a clinic waiting room won't type a structured vital-signs form on their phone. They'll speak: "Mom's BP was 132 over 80 this morning, she took her metformin." AI voice transcription has documented inaccuracies on medication names specifically[8], which is why we don't store the raw ASR text — the intake agent reads the transcript, classifies it into a structured vital_signs record, and reconciles drug names against the patient's actual medication list. To make this demo deterministic, we synthesize the spoken memo via this server's TTS endpoint (xai/grok-tts) first, then upload that exact audio bytes back through the intake pipeline (xai/grok-stt) so you can see the round trip.
What this proves: The pipeline is text→TTS→audio→ASR→intake-agent→structured records. The TTS and ASR endpoints are the same OpenAI-compatible gateway the operator already has configured for everything else. ASR transcription errors on medication names[8] are caught by the intake agent's classification + reconciliation pass, not stored as raw text.

✓ pass Patient chat — grounded Q&A with citations

Open-web medical RAG accepts misinformation: current systems have a limited ability to detect noise in retrieved documents[6]. We dodge that by giving the chat agent read-only access to Alice's actual records plus a household-curated wiki. When she asks "is my BP trending up?" the agent reads the vital_signs table, computes the trend, and answers with the specific dates it relied on. Physician trust of LLM-generated summaries depends on exactly this kind of verifiable reference[7].
What this proves: RAG-with-citations grounded in the household's own records is the architecture fix for two distinct problems: open-web misinformation acceptance[6] and the trust-via-citation requirement[7].

✓ pass Knowledge base — household-curated wiki, not open-web hallucination

When the chat agent is asked "is metformin safe with my other meds?", it shouldn't ask DuckDuckGo. The knowledge tab lets the household ingest sources they trust — a Mayo Clinic page, a NEJM perspective, a specialist's handout — into a Karpathy-style wiki. The chat agent consults that wiki only. Current medical-QA RAG systems have a documented inability to detect misinformation in open-web retrieval[6]; a curated wiki is the architecture fix.
What this proves: The wiki is the trust boundary. The household chooses what counts as a credible source; the chat agent stays grounded inside that boundary.

✓ pass Multi-channel capture — Telegram + WhatsApp settings

55% of US family caregivers handle injections, wound care, and medication management at home[1]. They're not at a desktop browser — they're in a clinic waiting room with a phone. Pair Telegram or WhatsApp to a family member and they can text "BP 130/82, just took my morning meds" or send a photo of a discharge summary the same way they'd text a friend. Both routes feed the same intake agent and produce the same structured records.

The regulatory + privacy case for self-hosted

HHS OCR's PHR Vendor Privacy Notice[11] states explicitly that consumer PHRs offered by non-covered entities fall outside the HIPAA Privacy Rule — governed only by the vendor's own privacy policies. The FTC's Health Breach Notification Rule (16 CFR Part 318, expanded April 2024) partially backstops this but doesn't eliminate the dependency. A self-hosted PHR keeps every byte under the household's control on their own machine, sidestepping third-party policy entirely.

ONC's 2015 Edition Certification + the 21st Century Cures Act mandated that EHRs expose patient-accessible APIs[9]. Mandl & Kohane (NEJM 2008, 2016) frame patient-controlled repositories as a "tectonic shift" inversion of the institutional record model[10] — the architecture this product implements.

Tech stack

Rust/Axum server, embedded SQLite (rusqlite + r2d2), Tera templates, vanilla JS — no SPA framework, no build step. AI calls go to OpenAI-compatible endpoints; local models via LM Studio / Ollama work identically. Telegram via long-poll, WhatsApp via Meta Cloud API webhook. ~12,000 lines of Rust + a few thousand of templates.
